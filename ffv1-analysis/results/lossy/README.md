# Lossy formats: regular_batch_060

Generated by `scripts/image_formats.py` from the analysis' report (`cargo run --release`, [`images.json`](../images.json)); do not edit. Part of [the comparison of the image formats](../README.md), whose charts show these formats next to the lossless ones.

These formats convert the frames, losing as little as the format allows: PNG holds only RGB, and JPEG holds YCbCr with the BT.601 matrix in full range, while the frames are YUV 4:2:0, BT.709 in limited range. They are the image formats every viewer opens. After writing each image from its FFV1 frame, the analysis reads it back, converts it back to the frame's YUV, and measures how far it is from its frame.

Converting from FFV1 is reading the FFV1 frame (45 ms) and writing the image. Each format's README has its charts, frame by frame: the size, the conversion, encoding and decoding time of each image, all the images against FFV1, and what each image lost.

## What they lose

How far the images read back are from their frames, over all the frames, plane by plane. The measures:

- **RMS error** (root mean square): for every sample, the difference between the image read back and its frame, in 8-bit levels; the differences are squared, averaged, and the average square-rooted. It is the typical error of a sample, a large error weighing more than a small one: 0 means exact, and 1 is the smallest step of an 8-bit sample, so an RMS error of 0.25 is a quarter of a step.
  How visible it is: the charts shade bands for it, from a viewer study (Afnan et al., *Sensors* 23(3):1297, 2023: 25 viewers flicker-compared images with JPEG versions of falling quality, and each one's threshold of seeing a difference was taken). Below 0.75 levels no viewer saw it on any image (*invisible in every test*), up to 2.01 the average viewer didn't (*invisible on average*), up to 5.74 the average viewer did (*visible on average*), and above, every viewer saw it on every image. They hold on average over an image: an RMS error says how big the error is overall, not where it is.
- **PSNR** (peak signal-to-noise ratio): the same error on a logarithmic scale, against the largest value of a sample, 255: 10 x log10(255² / RMS²) decibels. Higher is closer; it is infinite for an exact plane, and every 6 dB more halves the RMS error.
- **Largest error**: the sample furthest from its frame, in 8-bit levels.
- **Samples changed**: how many samples differ from their frame at all, by any amount.

## How far they look: butteraugli

**Butteraugli** is Google's perceptual metric: it models how the eye sees both images and finds, spot by spot, how visible their difference is. 0 means the two look identical. Unlike the RMS error, it weighs a difference by how visible it is: the same error counts more on a flat area than in fine texture, where the eye misses it. It compares RGB, so the frame and the image read back are both converted to linear RGB the same way, and differ only by what the format lost. Two norms sum up an image:

- **Max-norm**: the most visible difference anywhere in the image, its worst spot. Google's classes: below 1.0 *great* (no visible difference), 1.0 to 2.0 *acceptable*, above 2.0 *not acceptable*.
- **3-norm**: the differences over the whole image, a large one weighing more than a small one (libjxl's aggregate). Lower than the max-norm, it says how far the image is overall rather than at its worst spot.

The exact formats are their frames, byte for byte: their distance is 0, not measured.

## What it shows

- **PNG (16-bit RGB)**: 26.9 MiB per image, 5.5x larger than FFV1; converting an image from FFV1 takes 2955 ms, reading it back 246 ms (5.5x slower than FFV1). RMS error 0.00068 levels, butteraugli max-norm 0.1864 on average.
- **JPEG (quality 100)**: 8.7 MiB per image, 1.8x larger than FFV1; converting an image from FFV1 takes 322 ms, reading it back 124 ms (2.8x slower than FFV1). RMS error 0.240 levels, butteraugli max-norm 2.3162 on average.
- **Butteraugli puts the lossy formats in different classes**: PNG (16-bit RGB) scores 0.1864 on average, 0.0000 to 0.5273 (max-norm): *great* for every image; JPEG (quality 100) scores 2.3162 on average, 2.1119 to 2.5940 (max-norm): *not acceptable* for every image. Its max-norm is the single most visible spot of an image, so small errors spread over many samples leave a visible spot somewhere, while a few changed samples don't (PNG (16-bit RGB) changes 0.00% of its samples, by at most 1 levels; JPEG (quality 100) changes 5.74% of its samples, by at most 2 levels).
- **The two metrics disagree on JPEG (quality 100)**: by its RMS error, 0.240 levels, it is *invisible in every test* in the viewer study, while butteraugli's max-norm calls it *not acceptable*. They measure different things: the study's bands judge the error on average over the image, as its viewers saw whole images, while the max-norm judges the single worst spot, by a model of the eye meant to be strict. The truth is likely between: invisible to a viewer looking at the image, a difference a model or a close look at the right spot can find.
